Quantum Logic Lecture 2
The 4-Successor Quad-Tree, Complex Amplitudes & Wave Interference

“Welcome back!” Jack said as the class settled in.

Jill raised her hand: “Jack, in Bayesian probability, every possibility has a positive real probability P ≥ 0 on the 1D transect ℝ_ω. If there are two mutually exclusive ways for an event to happen, you just add them together: P_total = P₁ + P₂. Why on earth do quantum physicists need complex numbers with imaginary i?”

“That is the million-dollar question!” Jack beamed. “And the answer comes down to one word: Interference.”

1. Why 1D Real Numbers Are Not Enough

“In the classical world, probabilities only ever add up,” Jack explained. “If 10 particles go through Gate 1 and 10 particles go through Gate 2, you always detect 20 particles at the finish line.”

“At the atomic scale, however, matter and light behave like waves. Waves have crests (peaks) and troughs (valleys). When two waves collide crest-to-trough, they cancel each other out completely (destructive interference)!”

Jill thought for a second: “If probabilities are always positive numbers, you can never add two positive numbers together to get zero!”

“Exactly!” Jack said. “Nature does not keep track of probabilities directly. Nature keeps track of 2-dimensional amplitude arrows on our complex grid ℂ_ω!”

2. The 4-Successor Quad-Tree & The Complex Grid (ℂ_ω)

“In Module 3, we built the 1D real transect ℝ_ω using a 2-successor tree {-, +},” Jack recalled.

“To allow numbers to rotate, wave, and cancel in 2 dimensions, we upgrade to the 4-successor quad-tree:”

Quad-Tree Basis = { +1, -1, +i, -i }   (The 4 Compass Directions / Clock Angles)

“Every node on this 2D complex lattice is a complex number (a 2D vector arrow):”

z = x + i y = r · e^{i θ}
  • r = |z| = √(x² + y²) is the magnitude / length of the arrow.
  • θ is the phase angle (clock direction) of the quantum wave.

3. The Born Rule: From Complex Arrows to Real Probabilities

“If nature uses complex arrows z = x + iy, how do we ever measure real probabilities in a laboratory detector?” Jill asked.

“In 1926, physicist Max Born discovered the foundational bridge of quantum mechanics:”

The Born Rule:
The probability P of detecting an outcome with amplitude arrow z = x + iy is the squared length of the arrow:
P = |z|² = x² + y² = z · z*

“Because the squared length of any geometric arrow is always a real, non-negative number (|z|² ≥ 0), the Born Rule guarantees that every calculated probability is a completely valid positive number!”

4. Wave Interference: How Pathways Cancel

“Here is the quantum magic,” Jack said.

“When a quantum particle can reach a detector along two alternate pathways (like two slits in a screen), amplitudes add up as 2D geometric vectors first, and only then do we square the final vector to get the probability:”

z_total = z₁ + z₂  ⇒  P_total = |z₁ + z₂|²

Case A: Destructive Interference (Total Cancellation)

Suppose Path 1 has amplitude z₁ = +0.5 (pointing East) and Path 2 has amplitude z₂ = -0.5 (pointing West, 180° out of phase):

z_total = (+0.5) + (-0.5) = 0  ⇒  P_total = |0|² = 0% Probability! (Complete Darkness / Zero Clicks)

Case B: Constructive Interference (Reinforcement)

Suppose both pathways arrive perfectly in phase: z₁ = +0.5 and z₂ = +0.5:

z_total = (+0.5) + (+0.5) = 1.0  ⇒  P_total = |1.0|² = 100% Probability! (Maximum Signal)

Jill gasped: “So two open doors can cancel to zero because their complex arrows point in opposite directions and cancel out before we measure!”

“Precisely!” Jack cheered.

5. The Quantum State Vector

“On our hyperfinite complex grid ℂ_ω, a complete quantum state is simply a unit vector of amplitude arrows:”

|ψ⟩ = ( z₀, z₁, z₂, ..., z_{ω-1} )^T    with    ∑_{k=0}^{ω-1} |z_k|² = 1.000

“In Lecture 3, we will see what happens when a laboratory measurement observes this state vector — and discover that quantum measurement is simply vector projection!”
