Formal Statements: The Lecture

"Well hello all, look whose back, its Jack."

A collective grunt echoes through the room.

"Hey, its OK; its your secret if you can't stop thinking about propositions."

Jack grimaces and rubs his temples.

"OK; propositions are kind of boring. But don't worry. I am here to vanquish them to a small role on a much larger stage, but first..." Jack clears his throat "...I must set the stage."

Cue eye-rolling and a few audible groans.

"We all know what a collection is right; and many might have heard of set theory. Did you know not all collections are sets; some are too big. Sets can be really, really big, so this will not be a problem for us, and we can safely think of a set as a collection."

"But how can a collection be too big to be a set?" Jill asks, her face a picture of pure inquisitiveness.

"Hello, Jill, nice to see you. Well, a set is a mathematical term, a very important mathematical term. A set not only has a mathematical definition, it has a whole theory devoted to it. But as it turns out, not all collections are sets. Some are just too big to fit inside the precise mathematical definition. For example the collection of all sets is not a set. If you try to treat this collection as a set, paradoxes arise: you can draw contradicting conclusions; all very confusing. And I'm no expert Jill. Sometimes, at least until we have learned so for ourselves, we just have to trust established mathematical fact as fact."

"OK, so you just warned us about something we don't have to worry about?" Jill asks, triggering a wave of amused agreement around the room.

"Ha ha, I guess so Jill. But it does suggest a set is more than a collection. There are rules that define limits, even if for us those limits are far, far away and collections will always be sets. And per the jargon of some logicians, set will be a primitive notion, meaning we won't try to formally define or describe it: its our intuition of a collection. But we will formally describe two set constructors, namely the product and directed-pair constructors which construct sets from sets. So we will need some base sets for out constructors. A good example of a base set is the set of natural numbers. Another example is the binary set of Boolean truth values. Remember those, the possible values of a proposition?

A product is a set whose members are tuples. A tuple is a sequence of slots, but aside from identification, the order of slots doesn't matter. Each slot is bound to a set. The value of the slot will always be a member of the (bound) set. Multiple slots in a tuple may be bound to the same or different sets. More specifically, the product is the set of all possible such (bound) tuples. A product is denoted by the sets to which the tuple slots are bound. The times symbol is used to separate the sets. For instance if members of the product are tuples with two slots, each of which is bound to the natural numbers, denoted ℕ, then the product would be denoted by ℕ×ℕℕ\timesℕ."

"OK, but what has this to do with a product? Like multiplication, like in arithmetic?" Jill asks.

"Not much Jill. Worse, sometimes the sets are referred to as product factors. Maybe sometimes we just run out of words and reuse them? I don't know. But at least we now know what they're talking about right?"

A few murmured denials drift back from the back rows.

"But the product is used to help describe arithmetic. First though, we describe a directed-pair. A directed-pair is constructed from two sets; one is called the domain, the other the codomain. We use a directed-pair when we want to formally define a function. A function is a directed-pair and a rule that can assign to any member of the domain a member of the codomain. Using an arrow to separate the domain and codomain, both addition and multiplication of natural numbers can be described as the directed pair:

ℕ×ℕ→ℕℕ\timesℕ\toℕ

and a rule for adding or multiplying tuple values. Any questions?"

"Can a directed-pair be a domain or codomain?" Jill asks.

"Ah, good question. Perhaps so. Sounds like a good start for describing a function between functions. But for now, let's say we won't use directed-pairs for domains or codomains. I think that's a good idea; keep things simple."

A mostly puzzled but accepting nod goes around the room.

"Anyway, a set constructor product has nothing to do with multiplication. Its just a set constructor name. OK. We have our primitive notion of a set and two example sets: the natural numbers ℕ≡{1,2,…}ℕ\equiv\{1,2,\dots\} and the set of Boolean truth values 𝔹≡{true,false}𝔹\equiv\{\text{true},\text{false}\}. We have described two set constructors: product and directed-pair. We have a formal definition of function. When describing a function, we will often use the following convention: we provide a name or symbol identifier for the function followed, by a colon and the directed-pair, and considering a base set as a tuple with a single slot, we provide a domain tuple with each slot labeled by a variable, and a description of how these variables determine the codomain member where the tuple and description are separated by the ↦ symbol. For example, consider the addition of two natural numbers. Then we would have:

add:ℕ×ℕ→ℕ\text{add} : ℕ\timesℕ\toℕ (x1,x2)↦x1+x2(x_1, x_2)\mapsto x_1+x_2
