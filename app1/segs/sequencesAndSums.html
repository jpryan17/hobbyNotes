Sequences, Sums & Progressions

The doorway to continuous analysis: from the discrete stepping of trees to the algebra of ladders, musical scales, and telescoping sums.

Curricular Gateway: From Static Geometry to Dynamic Stepping

In our study of Geometry, we examined static spatial invariants: fixed vectors, angles, basis rotations, and metric distances. Analysis begins the moment we allow quantities to step. Before introducing rates of change or continuous motion, we examine the most fundamental discrete pattern: stepping through a sequence F(0), F(1), F(2), ..., F(n) and accumulating its differences.

📐

Demystifying F: The Sequence as a Formal Function Type

In casual speech, we often speak of a "sequence" as if it were a static list of numbers. But in the formal architecture of Middle Way Math (and Lean 4), every sequence F is a typed mapping characterized by two distinct layers:

1. Type Signature (Domain & Codomain)
F : ℕ_ω → ℝ_ω
The domain is the transfinite counting spine ℕ_ω = ℕ ∪ {ω}, and the codomain is the continuum ℝ_ω. This signature formalizes that indices step sequentially one-by-one from 0 toward the horizon ω.
2. Assignment Rule (Mapping Law)
k ↦ F(k)
The rule specifies the value assigned to each index k. This assignment rule can be defined recursively or sampled from a continuous function f(x) evaluated on discrete step indices:
  • Polynomial Progression: Sampling f(x) = c · x³ on index steps yields the cubic sequence F(k) = c · k³.
  • Exponential Progression: Sampling f(x) = a · r^x yields the geometric progression F(k) = a · r^k.
  • Physical Trajectory: Sampling kinetic energy f(v) = ½ · m · v² at velocity increments v_k = k · Δv produces the telescoping work-energy ladder.

1. The Two Fundamental Ladders of the Trees

In Middle Way Math, numbers are grounded in finite induction from Day 0. The two primitive inductive trees immediately give birth to two distinct modes of stepping:

Tree 1 (Unary Spine) 1-Successor Count

The Arithmetic Ladder (Linear March)

Formed by adding a fixed constant at each step. Models uniform motion, constant-force acceleration increments, and clock ticks.

Step Law & Explicit Formula
F(k + 1) = F(k) + d  ⇒  F(k) = a + k · d
Constant forward difference: ΔF(k) = d
• Geometric Picture: Equally spaced rungs climbing a straight vertical ladder.
• Continuous Limit: Forms the linear line y = m·x + b with constant slope d.
Tree 2 (Binary Branching) 2-Successor Branching

The Geometric Ladder (Multiplicative Scaling)

Formed by multiplying by a fixed ratio at each step. Models binary branch populations, radioactive decay, compounding, and sensory perception.

Step Law & Explicit Formula
F(k + 1) = r · F(k)  ⇒  F(k) = a · r^k
Difference proportional to term: ΔF(k) = F(k) · (r - 1)
• Geometric Picture: Each rung multiplies the height of the previous rung.
• Continuous Limit: Forms the exponential curve y = a · e^(k·x) where rate equals quantity.

2. Musical Scales: The Human Ear as a Geometric Processor

The geometric progression is not an abstract invention—it is hardwired into human sensory biology.

When we listen to music, our pitch perception is fundamentally logarithmic rather than linear. We perceive equal musical intervals (such as an octave, a fifth, or a semitone) when the physical sound frequencies form equal ratios, not equal differences.

The 12-Tone Equal Temperament Ladder

Acoustic Geometry

An octave is an exact frequency doubling: a ratio of 2 : 1. To divide the octave into 12 perceptually equal semitones (the 12 keys of an octave on a piano), the frequencies must advance by a constant multiplicative ratio r such that:

r¹² = 2  ⇒  r = 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.059463094...

Starting from standard concert pitch A4 = 440 Hz, the chromatic musical scale is literally a discrete geometric progression:

f_k = 440 · (2^(1/12))^k   Hz   (k = 0, 1, 2, ..., 12)
Step k Note Interval Frequency f_k Acoustic Ratio
0 A4 Unison 440.00 Hz 1.000
3 C5 Minor third 523.25 Hz 2^(3/12) ≈ 1.189
5 D5 Perfect fourth 587.33 Hz 2^(5/12) ≈ 1.335 (close to 4/3)
7 E5 Perfect fifth 659.26 Hz 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.498 (close to 3/2)
12 A5 Octave 880.00 Hz 2.000 (exact doubling)

After k = 12 steps, f_12 = 440 · (2^(1/12))^12 = 440 · 2 = 880 Hz, completing the octave at A5.

The Cochlear Frequency Sorter: Biology's Living Geometric Analyzer

Inside the inner ear, the basilar membrane physically separates sound frequencies along its length. High frequencies resonate near the stiff base, while low frequencies resonate near the flexible apex.

Because the spatial resonant frequency drops exponentially along the membrane, equal physical distances along the human sensor correspond to equal musical ratios—demonstrating that the geometric ladder is a biological architecture for processing reality.

3. The Sum of a Progression & The High School Trick

How do we sum a geometric progression? In secondary school algebra, students are introduced to the celebrated "shift-and-subtract" method:

S_n  =  a + a·r + a·r² + ... + a·rⁿ⁻¹
r·S_n =    a·r + a·r² + ... + a·rⁿ⁻¹ + a·rⁿ

Subtracting the second equation from the first collapses every single interior power:

(1 - r) · S_n = a - a · rⁿ  ⇒  S_n = a · (1 - rⁿ) / (1 - r)

Notice what made this calculation work: all intermediate terms cancelled pairwise. Only the initial boundary term a and the outer boundary term a · rⁿ survived.

4. Telescoping Sums: A Nod Toward the Fundamental Theorem

The shift-and-subtract trick for geometric series is not an isolated algebraic accident. It is a specific instance of a universal telescoping identity—an algebraic cancellation pattern that will soon serve as our bridge to continuous analysis.

The Universal Telescoping Cancellation

For any sequence function F : ℕ_ω → ℝ_ω—regardless of which algebraic rule F adopts—if we define the forward step difference:

ΔF(k) = F(k + 1) - F(k)

Then the sum of differences collapses identically to the net boundary difference:

∑_{k=0}^{n-1} ΔF(k) = [ F(1) - F(0) ] + [ F(2) - F(1) ] + ... + [ F(n) - F(n-1) ] = F(n) - F(0)

Because the theorem quantifies universally over ∀ (F : ℕ_ω → ℝ_ω), it holds for every possible rule! Whether we plug in the cubic rule F(k) = c · k³, the odd-sum rule F(k) = k², or the work-energy rule F(k) = ½ m v_k², the interior terms must vanish identically.

This finite algebraic identity—which in the analysis lectures ahead will blossom into the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)—is machine-verified in Lean 4 as MiddleWay.telescoping_ftc.

Numeric Sequences vs. Function Sequences

Here we observe an important architectural distinction that will recur throughout advanced analysis:

  • Numeric Sequence (ℕ_ω → ℝ_ω): At each step index k, the sequence outputs a single numerical scalar (a ladder rung, a partial sum, or a particle position).
  • Function Sequence (ℕ_ω → (ℝ_ω → ℝ_ω)): At each step index k, the sequence outputs an entire spatial function F_k(x) holding across the full continuum domain (such as progressive Taylor polynomial curves, Fourier wave sums, or heat diffusion time slices across a spatial bar).

Both advance along the discrete counting spine ℕ_ω, but a function sequence carries an entire spatial profile forward at every step.

5. From Step Index k to Continuum Transect x

Once we recognize that summation is boundary cancellation, continuous calculus requires only one conceptual refinement:

  1. Replace the integer step index k with grid nodes x_k = a + k · dx, where dx = 1/ω is an infinitesimal step.
  2. The difference quotient becomes the derivative rate: f(x_k) = ΔF(k) / dx.
  3. The telescoping sum becomes the definite hyperfinite Riemann integral:
    ∫_a^b f(x) dx = ∑_{k=0}^{N-1} f(x_k) dx = ∑_{k=0}^{N-1} ΔF(k) = F(b) - F(a)

Calculus is not an esoteric regime detached from elementary mathematics; it is the exact same pairwise boundary cancellation students learn in high school algebra, operating across an infinitesimal grid.
