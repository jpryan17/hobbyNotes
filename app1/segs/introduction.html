preface

Who am I to say? I am not an educator, or a mathematician, or a scientist, or an academic of any kind. I am merely an opinionated retiree with time on my hands for watching such inspiring videos as Leonard Susskind's theoretical minimum lecture series. My expertise in any of the subjects I pontificate on below, to put it politely, is of modest depth and considerable fragility. But I will continue as if I know what I am talking about. One might easily describe this effort as an academic exercise in it's least flattering sense. But the exercise is the thing.

The formal and natural sciences are complementary. Here science classifies both, and we refer to a curriculum for a basic scientific education. This page outlines a possible method for determining the topics in formal science and their order of presentation that would be included in the curriculum, and outlines an example method implementation.
We assume basic education has two main (knowledge related) responsibilities. First, provide a minimal level of education expected of all members of society. Second, provide students prerequisites for continuing studies required for future careers. In our case, the future careers are STEM-related, where STEM is an acronym for science, technology, engineering, and mathematics. It is relatively accurate to say on average only 25 percent of students will continue with STEM-related studies and careers. These students require a practitioner's knowledge of science, while 100 percent of students require a non-practitioner's knowledge of science. It is clear that the desiderata for curriculum design should include a complementary integration of these responsibilities.

 It is difficult to talk of the future of education without acknowledging the uncertainty of the role of AI, i.e. machine based intelligence. If such intelligent agency remains benign to indifferent, it is easy to imagine each child soon having a dedicated context used for interfacing with and individualizing educational resources. It is harder to imagine the social context of a basic education, which thankfully is well out of scope. But whatever the future, this context highlights our goal here: present one path among many that might be made available to future students.

It is difficult to describe this page if not a hallucination I share with LLM AI.

introduction

The first step in curriculum design is choosing a goal. Here we choose to maximize a student's knowledge of physical reality. This is by no means a practical choice. Without years of continuing education, this knowledge has no practical use. I have heard arguments that conclude only practical knowledge matters. As I am in no position to confirm or deny such arguments, it must be said that the chosen goal may require significant cognitive effort for no return. Yet we're relaxed; we are only exploring our options. But the exercise should include the requirement that this knowledge is complementary to the practical knowledge related to STEM-related careers.

We can describe the target knowledge in general terms. Specifically, it is not merely conceptual, it is formal.

No one theory describes physical reality. Perhaps the most important and inclusive theory, the standard model of particle physics, is out of reach. Just as well. The dynamics of particle interactions of high energy physics are no doubt fundamental to understanding physical reality, but are quite detached from everyday experience. General relativity is closer at hand; it also depends on a more limited and quite elegant geometric formalization. And it describes gravity, something we experience every day; but it only describes gravity.

We choose to formally describe quantum statistical mechanics.

We have our goal. The second step in curriculum design of formal science is to choose the foundation, where one starts. We choose classical logic and basic set theory as our foundation. The next step is determining the critical path, the one which has a minimal cognitive load, from formalization of foundational concepts to formalization of quantum statistical mechanics.

We describe core topics, non-core topics, and STEM-related topics. A core topic lies on the critical path, while a non-core topic, which still may be of general interest, does not. On the other hand, a STEM-oriented topic is often a requirement for future study. However, these classifications highlight dependency relations; one would hope each student is allowed to follow a path of their choosing. Additionally, there is no clear line to discriminate between topics designed for the general or STEM-oriented student.

Core topics are collected into general subjects areas, which are then presented in the specified order:
  • part I
    • propositional logic
    • formal statements
    • numbers
  • part II
    • Bayesian inference
    • quantum logic
    • quantum Bayesian inference

and we are done, since quantum Bayesian inference and quantum statistical mechanics share the same formal model.

With respect to the relation between the proposed curriculum and current curricula, the impact is proportional to the grade level. We assume a 0-12 grade basic education. Around grade 3-4 propositional logic would be introduced, and around grade 5-6 formal statements would be introduced. Once our formal definitions of numbers is introduced around grade 7-8, one would expect significant modification of topics in mathematics for the general student.

Even before children begin their basic education, they have mastered their native language. This entails recognition of logical (truth) connectives, as well as an understanding of the relationship between propositions and truth values. The role of propositional logic is not to introduce the student to novel concepts, as these have long since been mastered. It is the ground upon which we introduce formal science. And, we have now exposed the student to Boolean logic.

Formal statements implicitly reflect our foundational choices. We adopt the perspective axiomatized by ZF set theory. As the only type is set, we can use the language of classical single-sorted first order predicate logic. This means we want to expose the student to (simple) quantified predicate expressions. As a predicate is a function, and a function is between sets, the subject area introduces these topics as well.

Our definitions of numbers are key to limiting the cognitive load of the core path. Much of standard mathematics is built upon choices of a set's topology and sigma-algebra. These establish spaces one may describe as continuous and/or measurable. Our definitions of numbers provide a framework for nonstandard analysis and measure via discrete but transfinite arithmetic, bypassing continuous mathematics, which now (mostly) becomes a STEM-related topic.

We use nonstandard analysis to formally validate the mathematics of Bayesian Inference. Although we could now model classical statistical mechanics, we don't; rather we continue with our formal topics. We again use nonstandard analysis to validate quantum logic.

Retrofitting quantum for Boolean logic, we formalize quantum Bayesian inference.

We note nonstandard analysis, though helpful for formalizing mathematical concepts, usually falls short with respect to calculational efficiency. This is why continuous mathematics is required for future STEM-related study.
