The Product Constructor (×)

A product is a set whose members are tuples. A tuple is a sequence of slots. Aside from bookkeeping of which slot is which, the order of the slots doesn't change what the tuple is. Each slot is permanently bound to a specific set, and the value inside that slot will always be a member of that bound set. Multiple slots in a tuple may be bound to the same set or to different sets.

The product constructor builds the set of all possible such combinations.

A product is denoted by writing the sets side-by-side, separated by a multiplication symbol. For example, if members of the product are tuples with two slots, where both slots are bound to natural numbers, the product is denoted:
  • ℕ × ℕ
["OK, but what does any of this have to do with a product? Like multiplication in arithmetic?"]

Not much, Jill. To make things worse, mathematicians often refer to the sets inside a constructor as 'product factors.' Maybe we just run out of words sometimes and reuse old ones? I don't know. But at least now you know the secret code, right?

[Murmured denials.]

Don't worry, the product constructor is eventually used to help describe arithmetic. First though, we need to look at our second constructor: the directed-pair.

The Directed-Pair Constructor (→) & Functions

A directed-pair is constructed from two sets: one is called the domain, and the other is called the codomain.

We use a directed-pair when we want to formally define a function. A function is:
  • a directed-pair (Domain → Codomain)
  • and a rule that assigns to each member of the domain exactly one member of the codomain.
By using an arrow to separate the domain and codomain, standard addition or multiplication of natural numbers can be described as:
  • the directed pair: ℕ × ℕ → ℕ
  • accompanied by a rule for adding or multiplying those tuple values together.
["Can a directed-pair be a domain or a codomain itself?"]

Ah, phenomenal question Jill! Yes, it absolutely can. That is exactly how you start describing a function that processes other functions. But to keep things simple at our level, we enforce a house rule: we won't use directed-pairs as domains or codomains. It keeps our scaffolding nice and flat.

[Accepting nods.]

Let's establish a quick shorthand convention for writing a function. We write the name of the function, a colon, and its directed pair. Then, treating a single base set as a tuple with just one slot, we provide an input tuple labeled with variables, the mapping symbol ↦, and a rule showing how those variables determine the output.

For addition, it looks like this:
  • add : ℕ × ℕ → ℕ
  • (x₁, x₂) ↦ x₁ + x₂

The Predicate: Functions into 𝔹

Now we are in the perfect position to define the core building block of all formal science. Are you ready? Ta-da: The Predicate.

A predicate is simply a function whose codomain is locked to the set of Boolean truth values, 𝔹. If you feel a bit let down after all that buildup, good! A predicate is beautiful because it is that simple.

A classic example is the 'greater than' predicate, which compares two natural numbers:
  • GT : ℕ × ℕ → 𝔹
  • (x₁, x₂) ↦ { true if x₁ > x₂; false if ¬(x₁ > x₂) }
We can look at an even simpler predicate with only a single slot: checking whether a natural number is less than 10:
  • LT10 : ℕ → 𝔹
  • (x) ↦ { true if x < 10; false if ¬(x < 10) }
Crucially, a standalone predicate is neither true nor false. It's just an empty template waiting for inputs. However, we can ask questions about it: 'Is there some natural number less than 10?' or 'Are all natural numbers less than 10?'

The answer to the first question is a definitive yes (true), and the second is a definitive no (false). By asking these questions, we turn a flexible predicate back into a rigid proposition—a statement that is forced to be true or false.

The Content-Free Guide to Quantifiers & The Table of Strength

Well, we have to face them sooner or later; may as well make it sooner, right?

[Shifting weight and curious glances.]

We shall face the dreaded backward E and the upside-down A—those symbols for the highbrow phrases 'there exists' and 'for all':
  • The ∃ Symbol ("There Exists"): Means that there is some member of the predicate domain that evaluates to true.
  • The ∀ Symbol ("For All"): Means that every single member of the domain evaluates to true.
Logicians seem to be a rather stilted bunch, but they mean well, so we live with it.

Before we even hook these symbols up to real math rules like 'greater than' or 'less than', the mere sequence of these characters tells us exactly how 'heavy' a statement is. Here is our master cheat sheet for two variables, listed from the absolute strongest claim to the weakest:

Strength Tier Quantifier Order What it actually means Operational Reality
Tier 1 (Strongest) ∀x ∀y Everyone and everything: True for absolutely every possible pair. Unyielding
Tier 2 (Strong) ∃x ∀y The Master Key: One single, fixed choice works for every combination. Rigid
Tier 3 (Weak) ∀y ∃x Custom Fit: Everyone gets a match, but the choice shifts depending on the situation. Flexible
Tier 4 (Weakest) ∃x ∃y At least once: Minimum threshold. A single working pair exists somewhere. Permissive

Because the Master Key requires one fixed choice upfront, it automatically forces the Custom Fit scenario to be true. The logical implication goes strictly down the ladder—Tier 2 completely guarantees Tier 3 (∃x ∀y ⟹ ∀y ∃x)—but it never, ever goes backward.

Now, here is the catch: because this chart tries to make rules about all possible rules without caring about what's inside the brackets, logicians call it Second-Order Logic. It's the bird's-eye view of the math universe.

We aren't doing that here. We are taking Step 1.

Our curriculum uses First-Order Logic. In our sandbox, we don't make abstract statements about all possible rules. Instead, we keep our feet on the ground, pick a single, concrete relationship—like x > y—and watch how our variables move around inside a real, physical set of numbers.

But the cool thing is that this second-order chart remains our guide. By simply looking at the order of the ∀ and ∃ signs at the very front of your statement, you'll already know exactly how heavy your claim is before the math engine even turns on.

Our Sandbox: Built-In Domains & Predicates

Before we start chaining expressions together, let's open our sandbox toolbox and meet the specific mathematical domains and built-in predicates we have available to play with.

In our sandbox, all statements live on three foundational domains:
  • 1. The Base Set of Natural Numbers (ℕ): ℕ = {1, 2, 3, ...}. When we want an element variable, we write x₁, x₂, x₃, ... ∈ ℕ.
  • 2. The Power Set of Natural Numbers (𝒫(ℕ)): The collection of all possible subsets of ℕ. When we want a subset variable, we write y₁, y₂, ... ∈ 𝒫(ℕ).
  • 3. Constant Subsets in 𝒫(ℕ): Special, fixed subsets that already have a built-in definition, like GT5 = {x ∈ ℕ | x > 5} and LT10 = {x ∈ ℕ | x < 10}.
  • 4. The Boolean Set (𝔹): 𝔹 = {true, false} (or {1, 0}), which serves as the codomain for every predicate.
We have six primary predicates tailored to these domains:

Predicate Signature Template / Meaning Evaluation Rule
GT5 ℕ → 𝔹 GT5(x) (Single element) true if x > 5, else false.
LT10 ℕ → 𝔹 LT10(x) (Single element) true if x < 10, else false.
EVEN ℕ → 𝔹 EVEN(x) (Single element) true if x is even, else false.
GT ℕ × ℕ → 𝔹 GT(x₁, x₂) (Two elements) true if x₁ > x₂, else false.
LT ℕ × ℕ → 𝔹 LT(x₁, x₂) (Two elements) true if x₁ < x₂, else false.
∈ (Membership) ℕ × 𝒫(ℕ) → 𝔹 (x ∈ y) (Element in subset) true if x is a member of subset y.

Notice something subtle and neat: GT5 and LT10 can act both as unary predicates on an element (GT5(x)) and as constant subsets in membership predicates (x ∈ GT5). They are two sides of the exact same coin!

Constructing Formal Statements

Now let's examine how we build concrete mathematical statements from the ground up.

Just as we combined atomic propositions in propositional logic using Boolean connectives (¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔), we can combine predicates into predicate expressions, like:
  • P₁ ∧ P₂  |  P₁ ∨ ¬P₂  |  P₁ → P₂
However, a predicate expression by itself is not yet a proposition. A predicate has argument slots waiting for inputs.

["What actually goes inside a predicate?" Jill asks.]

Remember Jill, a predicate is fundamentally a function. It is defined on a directed pair:
  • Domain → 𝔹
where its domain is either a single set or a product of sets. If the domain is a single set, we treat it as a 1-slot tuple. If the domain is a product of sets, each product factor directly creates and types an argument slot:
  • The 1st product factor defines the type of slot 1.
  • The 2nd product factor defines the type of slot 2, and so on.
For example:
  • In GT5 : ℕ → 𝔹, the domain is a single set ℕ, giving us 1 slot typed to a natural number.
  • In GT : ℕ × ℕ → 𝔹, the domain is a 2-factor product, giving us 2 slots (slot₁, slot₂), each typed to ℕ.
We do not quantify a predicate directly, nor do we quantify a raw slot. Rather, we bind a variable (like x₁, x₂ ∈ ℕ) to each slot—and then prefix a quantifier to bind that variable.

Let's see this in action with our first live formal statement. Go ahead and click on this red statement on your screens, but don't get lost, there's a button up on top that will bring you safely back here:
  • ∃x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ]
There's a lot to look at right? But right now notice the expression you clicked on is displayed near the top and below that is a table that shows the quantified predicate GT5 to be true, and so the whole expression to be true. And of course it's true; there are loads of numbers greater than 5. If you need to, click on it again. You know how to get back.

["Yeah, click your heels twice." Chuckles.]

Well, Jill however you manage to get back, you might want to click on this next statement:
  • ∀x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ]
The table looks different as it should. I mean, not all numbers are greater than 5, right?

Notice what just happened: an open predicate function that was neither true nor false became an absolute proposition as soon as we bound its slot and quantified its variable!

Now, what happens when we combine multiple predicates into a single expression? The way we assign variables across the slots determines its logical meaning:
  1. Shared Variable (Joint Constraint):
    Click on this statement:
    • ∃x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ∧ LT10(x₁) ]
    Notice how both predicates share the same variable x₁. On your screens, you can see it evaluates to true because numbers 6, 7, 8, and 9 simultaneously satisfy both conditions (the intersection).
  2. Independent Variables (Separate Constraints):
    Now look at this one:
    • ∃x₁:ℕ, ∃x₂:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ∧ LT10(x₂) ]
    Here, x₁ and x₂ are independent variables. It evaluates to true trivially because we can choose x₁ = 20 (greater than 5) and x₂ = 2 (less than 10) without any conflict.
  3. Tautologies in Predicates:
    Just like in propositional logic, click on:
    • ∀x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ∨ ¬GT5(x₁) ]
    It is an absolute tautology because every number in ℕ is either greater than 5 or not.

Visualizing Truth: The Boolean Matrix

Now, let's take a closer look at the display widget below the table in the demo—the Boolean Matrix.

In propositional logic, we tested formulas using a static truth table with 4 or 8 rows. But in predicate logic over ℕ, there are infinitely many possible numbers. How can we develop a crisp intuition for why statements are true without getting lost in endless algebra? We look at their geometry on a sample of the domain.

Let's return to the first two quantified statements we clicked:
  • ∃x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ]
  • ∀x₁:ℕ [ GT5(x₁) ]
When GT5(x₁) is evaluated across our sample of natural numbers {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, the demo computes a 1D strip of Boolean values:
  • [ 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1 ]
In the matrix display, cells 1 through 5 are dim Grey (false), while cells 6, 7, and 8 are lit up in Blue (true).

Now, look at what the two quantifiers actually ask of this geometric strip:
  • The ∃ Quantifier ("There exists"): Asks, 'Is there at least one Blue light turned on in the strip?' Since cells 6, 7, 8 are lit up, the answer is true.
  • The ∀ Quantifier ("For all"): Asks, 'Is every single cell in the entire strip Blue?' Because cells 1 through 5 are grey, the answer is false.
Now, what happens when a predicate takes two variables, like GT(x₁, x₂) or LT(x₁, x₂)? The 1D strip naturally expands into a 2D Boolean Matrix (Grid):
  • The rows represent choices for x₁.
  • The columns represent choices for x₂.
  • Each cell (x₁, x₂) is lit up in Blue (1) if the relation holds, and dim Grey (0) if it fails.
This 2D matrix represents the raw, unquantified truth pattern of the relation across our sample domain.

Quantifier Alternation & The Table of Strength

Now that we understand the 2D grid widget, let's bring back our Second-Order Table of Strength from earlier and see how the order of alternating quantifiers (∀ and ∃) directly controls the geometry of truth!

When all quantifiers in a prefix are the same (∀x ∀y or ∃x ∃y), their order doesn't change the truth value of the statement. But when quantifiers alternate (mixing ∀ and ∃), their order fundamentally alters the meaning:

Let's test this directly on our screens. Go ahead and click on this statement:
  • ∀x₁:ℕ, ∃x₂:ℕ [ GT(x₂, x₁) ]
Look at the display that comes up. Near the top, the evaluation table confirms the statement is true. And below that, look at the interactive 2D Boolean Matrix.

Notice the triangular staircase of blue cells? Now check our quantifier claim: 'For every natural number x₁, there exists some number x₂ greater than x₁.'

This is our Tier 3 Custom Fit: for every row x₁ you pick, is there at least one blue light turned on in that row? Look across the grid: row 1 has lights at columns 2, 3, 4; row 2 has lights at 3, 4; row 3 has a light at 4. Every row gets a match!

Now, click on the exact same formula with the quantifiers flipped into Tier 2:
  • ∃x₂:ℕ, ∀x₁:ℕ [ GT(x₂, x₁) ]
Look at your screens. The evaluation flips to false.

Why? Because ∃x₂ ∀x₁ is the Master Key. For this statement to be true, you would need a single, solid column of Blue stretching from top to bottom across all rows! Scan the columns on your grid: column 1 has zero blue cells; column 2 has one; column 3 has two; column 4 has three. Not a single column is 100% solid blue.

["So a Master Key needs a solid line, but a Custom Fit just needs a light in every row?"]

Spot on, Jill! That is the geometric proof right in front of your eyes: having scattered blue lights can easily satisfy every row individually (Custom Fit), but it never produces a single, master column of 100% blue (Master Key). And a solid column automatically guarantees a light in every row it crosses—which is why Tier 2 completely forces Tier 3, but the implication can never go backward.

Power Sets & The Incidence Matrix

We can even test statements about subsets and the power set 𝒫(ℕ) using the set membership predicate ∈.

Click on this statement:
  • ∃x₁:ℕ, ∀y₁:𝒫(ℕ) [ (x₁ ∈ y₁) ]
Look at the matrix now! For the four base numbers ℕ₄ = {1, 2, 3, 4}, there are 2⁴ = 16 possible subsets. The demo displays a 4 × 16 Power Set Incidence Matrix, where each column represents a different subset (from the empty set ∅ all the way to {1, 2, 3, 4}).

The statement asks: 'Is there some number x₁ that is inside EVERY subset y₁?'

Look at the very first column of your grid—that's the empty set ∅. It has no members, so its entire column is grey. Because no number is in the empty set, no row can be solid blue, and the statement evaluates to false.
