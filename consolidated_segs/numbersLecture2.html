Lecture 2:
tree representations of numbers
and recursive definitions for the 2-successor (inductive definition) case

Howdy folks.

Some of you might feel that my first lecture on numbers was kind of, ah, abstract. So I'm here to make excuses about that. I'm kidding. Mostly; after all, I was describing formal definitions, which of themselves have no practical use. But I'm ready to make amends, to make things concrete. Sound good?

[mostly puzzled looks, mild agreement.]

There seems to be a deep connection between numbers and balanced trees, i.e. a graph structure with a single root and a constant branching number. In fact, the branching number corresponds to the number of successors identified by the inductive definition of the number set.

Of course, recursive definitions of some level of order and arithmetic-like operations need to be defined over the tree. Mathematician have shown this can only be done for specific numbers: ∀x₁ ∃x₂ [ PG(x₁,x₂) ], and the further out you go, the less useful the operations. We do not explore any further than four, but not to our surprise, some mathematicians certainly have, and physicists, for example, find some of their findings useful. But we stick to the basics: 1, 2 and 4.  

As it is so often, the tree for the single successor case is rather boring. It is like a trunk without branches reaching ever upward; or an endless road with no side-roads, stretching toward the horizon; either way, signpost after signpost, little nodes: ∀x₁ ∃x₂ [ PG(x₁,x₂) ]; boring.

The 2-successor case corresponds to a binary tree. Descriptions of the tree representation of numbers will focus on the the 2-successor case, with only minimal discussion of what I suspect - I possess limited knowledge of the subject - is a rich environment to explore complex numbers.

A tree representation of a 1, 2, or 4 successor inductively defined number system is primarily characterized by a cutoff birthday for displayed numbers. As mathematicians have shown rather than assumed, this corresponds to a (finite) display of natural, dyadic rational, and Gaussian dyadic rational numbers, respectively.

The 1-successor case not only serves as a trunk; in the 2-successor case it serves as a branch. (It may be interesting to consider whether there is a difference between viewing the branches of the 4-successor case as the 2-successor case, rather than a pair of 1-successor cases.)

Since the birthday view is essential to recursive definitions, and natural for a small set of displayed numbers, it will generally be our perspective of tree representations. However before that, we might attempt to summarize a wider perspective using the notion that each branch of each 2-successor tree node is a 1-successor that does not intersect (share nodes) with any other tree node.
