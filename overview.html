Formalizing Popular Science Using Middle Way Mathematics to Describe Quantum Statistical Mechanics

Popular science routinely asks readers to appreciate modern physical theories—such as quantum mechanics, relativity, and thermal physics—through qualitative prose, metaphorical analogies, and informal illustrations. When curious readers seek the underlying mathematical logic, they are immediately confronted by an academic barrier: decades of technical prerequisites in classical differential calculus, epsilon-delta limit theory, and measure-theoretic analysis.

Middle Way Mathematics (MWM) formalizes the conceptual foundation of popular physical science directly. It provides a mathematical runway—from single-sorted logic, recursive number trees, and infinitesimals to state spaces, entropy, and quantum density operators—without  hand-waving and metaphors.

Across the lecture series, the dialogues trace a deliberate developmental progression: in the early lectures, Jill explores logic and numbers as an inquisitive child in elementary school; by the final series on quantum statistical mechanics, she is engaging with modern physics at a university level.

This arc directly reflects the conceptual architecture of Middle Way Mathematics:

  • The Foundations Are Simple: Starting with binary truth, counting spines, and Conway number trees requires no advanced academic preparation—the root ideas are so transparent that a young learner can grasp them directly through intuition and interactive exploration.
  • The Conceptual Sophistication Escalates Naturally: As the lectures advance into rates of change, Bayesian probability spaces, and non-commutative quantum density operators, the material matures in lockstep with the inquirer.

Middle Way Mathematics (MWM) is assumed to model a slice of an assumed mathematical reality which is assumed to model aspects of an assumed physical reality. We side with Einstein: the moon really is there. We recognize that our relationship to physical reality is phenomenological, and if disciplined enough, empirical and quantitative. We acknowledge any relationships we assume to hold between phenomenological observables is subject to change. We assume mathematics is a formal science. A formal science is a set of suppositions, and rules for describing their implicational canopy. It is all supposition. There is no need to guess.

Improvements to physical theories often mirror improvements to the underlying mathematical representations. For example, over four millennia the concept of angle has been formulated as a builder's slope, a geometric chord, a half-chord (sine), an arc length (radian), and an algebraic rotor. None of these is the physical phenomenon of turning itself; each is a mathematical tool suited to specific analytical tasks. In MWM, representing numbers and angles via directed paths on recursive trees is chosen because it enables exact, discrete calculation while remaining conceptually transparent.

2. The Evolution of Physical State Spaces

Over the past four centuries, mathematical physics advanced by shifting where physical reality is modeled to reside—progressing from direct spatial geometry to abstract, high-dimensional state spaces:

Historical Era Where Reality Is Modeled to Live State Representation
1. Direct Physical Space
(Newton, 17th c.)		 3D Euclidean Space: ℝ³ × ℝ Point particles with definite positions (x, y, z) evolving along continuous trajectories governed by vector forces.
2. Abstract State Spaces
(Lagrange & Hamilton, 18–19th c.)		 Configuration & Phase Spaces: Q × P The state of an entire physical system is a single point moving through an abstract energy phase space of positions and conjugate momenta.
3. Statistical Ensembles
(Boltzmann & Gibbs, 1870s)		 Probability Distributions over Microstates Macroscopic thermodynamic observables (temperature, pressure, entropy) emerge as statistical averages over microstate distributions.
4. Explanation Spaces
(Bayes, Boltzmann, Jaynes)		 Sample Space (Ω) & Belief Distributions Science is formalized as reasoning under uncertainty: candidate hypotheses assign probability distributions over states, updated via evidence.
5. Quantum State Space
(Planck, Born, von Neumann)		 Complex Hilbert Space (ℋ) & Density Operators (ρ) Physical states are complex probability amplitude vectors or statistical density operators satisfying unitary evolution and projection rules.

3. The Evolution of Mathematical Analysis 

 Mathematical analysis also evolved:

  • Intuitive Infinitesimals (17th–18th Century) — Leibniz, Newton, Euler:
    Calculus was invented using infinitesimals (dx, dy)—quantities strictly greater than zero yet smaller than any positive standard real number. Derivatives were algebraic ratios (dy / dx) and integrals were genuine sums of microscopic rectangles (∑ y · dx).
  • The Epsilon-Delta Approach (19th Century) — Cauchy, Weierstrass, Dedekind:
    Infinitesimals were banished due to a lack of formal foundations. They were replaced by static limit definitions involving epsilon and delta (ε–δ). While rigorous, this framework severed calculus from its intuitive operational roots and erected substantial pedagogical barriers.
  • The Topological Escape (Early–Mid 20th Century) — Hausdorff, Lebesgue, Bourbaki:
    Continuous mathematics shifted into point-set topology and abstract measure spaces. This provided powerful generality for functional analysis, but further distanced mathematics from constructive calculation.
  • Nonstandard Analysis — Abraham Robinson:
    Infinitesimals were given rigorous foundations via Robinson's nonstandard analysis.

4. Conway number trees and the middle way

An inductive definition is an important tool in mathematical reasoning. For finite induction, the inductive steps are the natural numbers. For general (transfinite) induction, the inductive steps are the ordinal numbers. Note the natural numbers correspond to the ordinal numbers that are less than ω.

A set defined by a general inductive definition supports recursive definitions. MWM uses 1-successor, 2-successor, and 4-successor inductive definitions to define 3 classes of numbers:

  • in the 1-successor case, the self-reflective class of ordinal numbers Ord
  • in the 2-successor case, the Conway number tree, the class of (so called surreal) numbers No
  • and the 4-successor case, corresponding to two 2-successor cases.

A recursive definition of a well order in the 1-successor case is foundational in the history of mathematics, and is highly resonant with intuition. However, it was only a decade or so after Robinson's work on nonstandard analysis that John Conway described recursive definitions for a total order and field level arithmetic operations of addition, and multiplication for the 2-successor case. (We might surmise that these definitions are not quite so intuitive as for the 2-successor case.) In the 4-successor case, (although no total order may be defined), mainly because of it's correspondence with two 2-succesor cases, field level arithmetic operations of addition, and multiplication can also be recursively defined.

But general induction define classes, not sets. The defined collection must be restricted to a collection with less members, to a set. In the 1-successor case, we need only limit the number of steps to a given ordinal number, say α; the collection of ordinal numbers less than α is denoted Ord(α). For example if α=4, then Ord(α)≡{1,2,3}. The set of natural numbers ℕ is defined by finite induction. The set ℕ corresponds to Ord(ω), where ω is the supremum of ℕ.

In the 2-successor case, the steps required to define a number is referred to as the number's birthday. Note the birthday is an ordinal number. A birthday is a convenient means of restricting the collection No. For a given ordinal α, No(α) denotes all numbers in No whose birthday is less than α. 

In a similar manner, based on the correspondence with two 2-successor cases, the class generated in the 4-successor case may likewise be restricted to a set.

4.1 Logical Framework

MWM uses classical  (i.e. inclusive of the law of excluded middle) single-sorted first order predicate logic, standard ZF set theory, four primitive base sets, and two fundamental constructors:

  • 𝔹 ≡ {0, 1}: Boolean truth values.
  • ℕ_ω ≡ Ord(ω+1): The ordinal numbers that are less or equal to ω.
  • ℝ_ω ≡ No(ω+1): The set of numbers whose birthday is less or equal to ω.
  • ℂ_ω: The 2D complex grid (ℝ_ω × ℝ_ω).
  • Constructors: Cartesian product (×) and directed pair (→).

4.2 The Cornerstone Axiom

Rather than constructing transfinite numbers from axiomatic set-theoretic scratch, MWM asserts the algebraic field property established by Conway, Gonshor, and van den Dries & Ehrlich:

(No(ε₀), +, ·) is a totally ordered algebraic field

where:

  • The carrier set No(ε₀) consists of all nodes on the 2-successor number tree whose tree depth (birthday) is strictly less than Cantor's ordinal ε₀ = sup { ω, ω^ω, ω^(ω^ω), ... }.
  • Order (≤) and arithmetic operations (+, ·) are defined recursively with respect to the node's birthday.
  • No(ε₀) is non-Archimedean; it contains both transfinite numbers and infinitesimals. It serves as the ambient field for all mathematical expressions in MWM.

A distinction is often drawn between constructive and non-constructive mathematics. MWM uses both perspectives, top down and bottom up, and they meet in the middle, at omega. The approach is illustrated in the following diagram.

MWM assumes (No(ε0), +, ·) is the first emergent mathematical field, as established via a nonconstructive logical formalism. However, the inductively defined number trees are constructions. 

In MWM, the set ℝ_ω is not defined as a carrier for any algebraic structure; rather, numbers in ℝ_ω are the numbers used in MWM algebraic expressions. Note that the result of the addition or multiplication of numbers in ℝ_ω is not necessarily a member of ℝ_ω, but it will be a member of No(ε0); i.e.

  • + : ℝ_ω ×  ℝ_ω → No(ε0)
  • ·  :  ℝ_ω ×  ℝ_ω → No(ε0)

4.3 The Precision Evaluation Map

To evaluate an algebraic expression E_No+1 whose terms belong to No(ω+1), MWM defines an explicit truncation function:

eval: E_No+1 × ℕ → E_𝔻

In MWM, an algebraic expression is a formal statement that includes numeric terms which are labels of members of ℝ_ω. The evaluation map transforms a formal expression with numeric terms into a formal expression whose numeric terms are dyadic rationals of a given precision p ∈ ℕ, where each number term is replaced by it's 2-successor tree address, truncated to p directional branches, yielding an exact element of 𝔻. We note:

  • there is a subset ℝ_ non-Archimedean  ⊂ ℝ_ω such that  ℝ_ non-Archimedean  is isomorphic to ℝ. 
  • However, ℝ_ω also includes ω and 1/ω
Thus in addition to resolving a numeric term like 1/3 or π to a dyadic rational of a certain precision, the eval function must be able to handle transfinite and infinitesimal numbers in No(ε0).

4.4 MWM is a constructive nonstandard analytical approach to mathematics

MWM is a constructive approach to mathematics, but not in a dogmatic sense; rather, number sets are constructed. The infinitesimal, essential to a nonstandard approach, is just another constructed number. The ontological status of constructed numbers 1/3, π, and 1/ω are identical: they are all numbers whose birthday is ω.

4.5 The fundamental isomorphism

However, none of this would be of much use if an inductive argument didn't establish the mathematical fact that 
  • (No(ω), +, ·) ≅ (𝔻, +, ·) 
where the arithmetic operations in (No(ω), +, ·) are inherited from (No(ε0), +, ·) and (𝔻, +, ·) is the Euclidean ring of dyadic arithmetic, which is the realm of MWM calculation.

4.7 The 4-Successor Tree: 2D Complex Plane & Infinitesimal Area

While 1D arithmetic is modeled along a 2-successor binary tree ({+1, -1}), 2D geometry is modeled on a 4-successor quadtree branching along orthogonal directions ({+1, -1, +i, -i}):

  • At finite stages (up to Day ω), the tree spans the Gaussian dyadics 𝔻[i] = { a + b·i | a, b ∈ 𝔻 }.
  • At stage ω+1, orthogonal branching establishes infinitesimal resolution in both axes: dx = 1/ω and dy = 1/ω.
