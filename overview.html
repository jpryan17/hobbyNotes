A General Education in Mathematics The Middle Way

The website middlewaymath.app describes curriculum and content for a general education in mathematics. This site is maintained by a hobby-level project, which is under constant modification. The page must be considered "unvetted" information as the "hobbyist" has neither bona fides nor actual expertise, only opinions.

A general education targets all students. Its explicit intent is not to prepare students for further study in mathematics; rather its purpose is to provide an overview of the role of mathematics in mathematical physics and the modern world. In fact, one expects that a general education will not provide the practical knowledge some students will require even for further mathematical studies. If we, without much precision, categorize knowledge as practical, formal, or conceptual, then we may describe the curricular target as moving the needle from conceptual to formal.

Mathematics is assumed to be a formal science. This assumption provides the page's perspective on the ontological status of mathematics: it lives in a world of supposition, a bridge that connects our intuition with our experience, a world where we ask "what if?", and see where it leads, and, eventually, how well it can fit.

However, this ontological stance does not preclude the possibility that the formalism models something. In fact, this is assumed by the page. It allows Middle Way Mathematics to be described as an effective model for a pedagogic description of modern mathematics.

The hallmark of any formal science is that it is expressed formally, not naturally, although the formality will be embedded in explanation. The formality a mathematical community adopts will impact its school of thought. The page assumes an unadorned single-sorted First-Order Logic (FOL) and Zermelo–Fraenkel (ZF) set theory as its formal foundation. That is, the page adopts about the simplest formal foundation possible. But it is important to note that the page makes no claim that this formalism has the expressive power to model all of mathematics.

The Foundational Axiom: Ambient Field Closure at No(ε₀)

An effective model of mathematics takes as an axiomatic foundation an established mathematical fact. In the case of Middle Way Mathematics, this axiomatic foundation is the mathematical fact, fully established in 2001, that:

Foundational Axiom: (No(ε₀), +, ·) is an algebraic field.

To describe the axiom, we type ε₀ as an ordinal. An ordinal is a primitive type. Here too, the model becomes effective, as ordinals have been modeled by using von Neumann sets as class partition instances of (set, well-order) pairs. We merely assume the existence of a well-ordered spline that includes the natural numbers starting this spline, which also includes the supremum of ℕ, i.e. ω, and ε₀.

No is the class of nodes of a tree defined by 2-successor transfinite induction; No can be constructed in various ways. The page uses the ordinals for birthday values. Either implicitly or explicitly, each tree node, i.e. number, has a birthday.

One can consider all tree nodes whose birthday is less than some cutoff ordinal α; this is denoted No(α). The field-level operations + and · are defined by transfinite recursive definitions originally described by John Conway, and No will sometimes be referred to as the Conway number tree.

What the foundational axiom claims is that there is an ordinal ε₀, greater than ω, which is the first ordinal which, when used as a cutoff, identifies an algebraic field: the ambient field for arithmetic. We do not use this field directly; ε₀ may be unimaginably small by ordinal standards, but from a computational perspective, it is unimaginably large. However, we are now assured that any subset of No(ε₀) will inherit field-level arithmetic operations.

📜 Historical Development: From Conway to Gonshor to van den Dries & Ehrlich (1976–2001)

The recognition of No(ε₀) as the natural ambient field for arithmetic represents the culmination of a quarter-century of research in transfinite algebra and model theory:

1976 — John H. Conway (On Numbers and Games): Conway introduced the surreal numbers No and demonstrated that the full collection forms a proper-class real-closed field containing both the real numbers and the transfinite ordinals. However, because No as a whole is a proper class rather than a set, standard mathematical foundations and computational proof assistants require well-founded, set-sized subfields.
1986 — Harry Gonshor (An Introduction to the Theory of Surreal Numbers): Gonshor reformulated Conway numbers using transfinite sign-expansions (sequences of + and - steps). He proved foundational algebraic results for initial segments, identifying that certain cutoff subsets behave as ordered algebraic structures and exploring early conditions for subfields. However, the precise ordinal boundary where a birthday-truncated initial segment No(α) forms an authentic, self-contained subfield remained an open question.
2001 — Lou van den Dries & Philip Ehrlich ("Fields of surreal numbers and exponentiation", Fund. Math. 167): Logicians Lou van den Dries and Philip Ehrlich published the definitive classification theorem: The birthday-truncated surreal numbers No(α) form an elementary subfield of No if and only if α is an ε-number (an ordinal fixed point satisfying ω^α = α). Because Cantor's ordinal ε₀ = sup { ω, ω^ω, ω^(ω^ω), ... } is the very first non-zero ε-number, No(ε₀) is proven to be the unique minimal set-sized subfield of the Conway surreal numbers!

This historical milestone justifies Middle Way Mathematics adopting (No(ε₀), +, ·) as its foundational roof: it provides the minimal rigorous boundary where arithmetic is guaranteed algebraic closure without requiring a proper class.

The Primary Subsets: Numbers, Continua, and the 2D Plane

Middle Way Mathematics identifies three essential stages along this ordinal spline:

1. No(ω) ≡ 𝔻: Finite Birthdays and the Dyadic Arithmetic Ring

As ω is the first non-finite ordinal, No(ω) identifies the set of numbers with finite birthdays. Two foundational facts are established:

  • The recursive arithmetic definitions that hold for the whole tree, via transfinite induction, are described by pure finite induction for numbers whose birthdays are finite; and
  • (No(ω), +, ·) is isomorphic to (𝔻, +, ·), where operations for No(ω) are Conway's recursively defined tree operations, and (𝔻, +, ·) denotes standard dyadic arithmetic.

In Middle Way Mathematics, all numerical calculation occurs within the realm of the ring of dyadic arithmetic.

The Core Dyadic Isomorphism: While Conway's tree-inductive recursive definitions provide foundational ontological purity (constructing numbers out of simpler ancestral option sets { L | R }), using recursive option evaluations for actual computation would be extremely impractical. For instance, computing a sum like 1/2 + 3/4 via tree options requires dozens of combinatorial recursive calls across earlier birthdays. The Core Dyadic Isomorphism proves that every finite tree path evaluates directly to an exact dyadic fraction m / 2^k, enabling the host machine to execute addition, subtraction, and multiplication in O(1) time via integer arithmetic and binary bit-shifts on CPU registers, without losing a single drop of precision.

2. ℝ_ω ≡ No(ω+1): The Pedagogical 1D Continuum

For Middle Way Mathematics, a 1D (non-complex) number is a member of No(ω+1), where page notation assumes ℝ_ω ≡ No(ω+1). The notation ℝ_ω suggests that, in Middle Way Mathematics, ℝ_ω plays the role of the real numbers in standard mathematical curricula. But ℝ_ω is not the classical real continuum. It is often said that ℝ ⊂ ℝ_ω; but no such claim is made: ℝ allows a grid size of zero, which is made sense of only by a theory of limits, a theory of continuity in an Archimedean field. ℝ_ω is what it is: nodes on the Conway number tree whose birthday is less than or equal to ω.

An interesting consequence is that, for Middle Way Mathematics, the ontological status of 1/3 and 1/ω are equivalent. Both are numbers whose birthday is ω. Another thing to note is that:

  • (ℝ_ω, +, ·) is not a field, as it is not closed under arithmetic operations.

For example, multiplying two base infinitesimals (1/ω · 1/ω = 1/ω²) produces a number born on Day ω+2, which lies beyond ℝ_ω. However, the result of such operations will be a member of No(ε₀) and so will be a perfectly valid Conway tree number; it just happens the result is not in ℝ_ω. In this sense, we indirectly deal with numbers in No(ε₀): we express mathematical statements using numbers in ℝ_ω. An actual number might not lie in ℝ_ω, but it is certainly a member of No(ε₀).

Since ℝ_ω is not used for numerical calculation—as stated above, all calculation is with respect to the ring (𝔻, +, ·)—the fact that ℝ_ω is not closed with respect to these operations is not particularly relevant. What is highly relevant is that, when mathematical structure is described using ℝ_ω, the description can include infinitesimal values with as much confidence as using π or 1/3.

3. The 2D Planar Jump: Quadtrees (Tree4) and the Complex Horizon ℂ_ω

The 2-successor binary tree Tree2 sweeps out the 1D real continuum like a polar flashlight beam, but it possesses an inherent 180° half-space blindspot: it cannot natively rotate around an axis or represent 2D planar space without invoking square roots of negative numbers.

To model physical geometry, wave mechanics, electromagnetism, and rotational kinematics, Middle Way Mathematics generalizes the branching spine to the 4-successor quadtree (Tree4). Each node branches along the four cardinal compass directions:

QuadDir ≔ { East (+1),  West (-1),  North (+i),  South (-i) }

On finite birthdays k < ω, paths on Tree4 evaluate to the Gaussian Dyadics:

𝔻[i] ≔ { (a + b · i) / 2^k  ∣  a, b ∈ ℤ,  k ∈ ℕ }

At the limit horizon, Tree4(ω+1) generates the complex horizon ℂ_ω. Here, planar geometry and phase rotation operate directly through discrete directed pairs, eliminating the mysterious "imaginary" black box of standard algebra.

Calculus Without Limits: The Infinitesimal Derivative in Action

The greatest pedagogical dividend of this architecture is that differential calculus is rescued from the ε-δ limit thicket and returned to direct algebra.

With the base infinitesimal differential dx ≔ 1/ω, a student calculates the derivative of a function like f(x) = x² directly by finite difference:

[x + dx]² - x²  =  (x² + 2x·dx + dx²) - x²
               =  2x·dx + dx²

Dividing by dx:    (2x·dx + dx²) / dx  =  2x + dx

There is no dividing by zero, no pretending that dx is non-zero during division and then magically zero a moment later. The quotient 2x + dx is an exact member of No(ε₀). To obtain the observable, physical slope, one simply applies the standard part projection:

st(2x + dx)  =  2x  ∈  𝔻

The infinitesimal tail dx = 1/ω drops away under standard part observation, leaving the exact derivative 2x. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus becomes a telescoping sum of finite rectangles, and integration becomes exact summation.

Trigonometry & Discrete Rotors: The Ptolemaic & CORDIC Tree

In standard general education, circular functions like sin(θ), cos(θ), and e^x are typically introduced either as opaque calculator buttons or as infinite Taylor series that non-majors never truly grasp.

Middle Way Mathematics constructs trigonometry through Ptolemaic chord laws and CORDIC bisection directly on the dyadic tree. An angle is not a continuous mystery; a dyadic angle is a certified rational fraction θ = m / 2^k born on day k. The sine and cosine of these angles are computed by successive half-angle root bisections:

sin²(θ / 2)  =  (1 - cos(θ)) / 2

Planar rotation is achieved by multiplying by unit rotors on the Gaussian dyadic grid 𝔻[i]. Because every trigonometric value is bounded by certified bisection intervals, students can compute and visualize rotational physics without black-box approximations.

The Interactive Simulation Sandboxes (BTD, BID, TTD)

Middle Way Mathematics is not merely a philosophical manifesto or a static textbook; it is built as an interactive visual laboratory delivered directly to students through specialized interactive demo engines:

Interactive Learning Engines in Middle Way Math:
  • BTD (Binary Tree Demo): Allows students to interactively explore Conway tree branching, birthday generations d < ω, and the tree order geometry in real time.
  • BID (Binary Interval Demo): Allows students to zoom continuously into interval subdivisions, witnessing how dyadic intervals [m/2^k, (m+1)/2^k] drill inward toward the infinitesimal horizon dx = 1/ω.
  • TTD (Truth Table Demo): Provides an interactive truth-table deduction sandbox, verifying propositional logic and classical equivalences before algebraic numbers are introduced.

Why Equality is Directed: Mathematical Statements as Computational Stencils

In classical mathematics, equality is defined symmetrically: A = B ⟺ B = A. While mathematically pure, this static view obscures how mathematics functions in physics, engineering, and computer science.

In computation and the real world, an equation is an asymmetric operational transformation: given a set of known inputs, execute a rule to produce an updated output state. Middle Way Mathematics formalizes this by replacing static equations with Directed Equalities:

  • A Directed Equality expresses a mathematical relationship as a certified computational stencil.
  • It pairs a formal mathematical theorem in Lean 4 with an executable Rule Interpreter (a block of structured pseudocode).
  • The rule interpreter assigns an exact member of the codomain to any input in the domain using the host machine's native dyadic engine.
Summary of the Architecture:
  • This allows limit theory to be replaced by direct algebraic manipulation; and
  • A standard part function is established that defines a unique dyadic rational for any finite number in No(ε₀). For any finite number, this standard part projection is realized concretely by finite prefix truncation and Conway Cut bisection down to day k < ω.

This approach achieves a clean, permanent separation of algebraic manipulation (in ℝ_ω and ℂ_ω) and numeric calculation (in 𝔻 and 𝔻[i]).

Dual-Engine Formal Verification in Lean 4

Middle Way Mathematics uses Lean 4 to verify its formal statements. The project organizes its formal codebase into two complementary source files:

  • The Semantic Scaffold (Noncomputable): Formal statements describing the axioms, definitions, and theorems of Middle Way Mathematics. This is where the primitive type ordinal, the fundamental axiom that No(ε₀) is an algebraic field, and the limit horizons (ℝ_ω, ℂ_ω, and infinitesimals dx = 1/ω) are described.
  • The Computational Corner (Computable): Formal statements underlying concrete numeric calculation. This file implements dyadic arithmetic in a manner that exactly matches the formal description of Middle Way Mathematics: the number sets (Tree1, Tree2, Tree4) are defined by finite structural induction, while order and arithmetic operations are certified as an authentic commutative ring (𝔻, +, ·) with zero axioms.

The current Middle Way Mathematics website hosts a static page without a live runtime connection to Lean. Instead, the project includes a development environment with Node-based utilities that use a local installation of Lean to compile and cache information for the application. For this reason, the application does not rely on live Lean calculation of dyadic arithmetic. This is a minor inconvenience because, as established by the Core Dyadic Isomorphism, recursive definitions on trees are computationally mirrored by the host machine's native arithmetic. Nevertheless, the codebase includes rules to implement the recursive definitions, primarily to demonstrate their equivalence with dyadic arithmetic and to illustrate their calculational inefficiency.

The primary arithmetic engine built into the application is a dyadic arithmetic machine that operates on the nodes of the Conway number tree whose birthdays are finite. This is the engine used by the application's rule interpreter, where a rule is a block of pseudocode responsible for assigning a member of the directed pair's codomain to any member of the directed pair's domain in a directed equality.
