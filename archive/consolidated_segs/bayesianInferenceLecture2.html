Lecture 2: Sequential Updating & The Odds Formulation
Evidence Streams, Bayes Factors & The Base-Rate Fallacy

1. The Golden Rule: Today's Posterior is Tomorrow's Prior

In science and engineering, evidence rarely arrives as a single, isolated event. Detectors continuously stream readings, doctors run multiple tests, and navigation systems constantly poll sensors.

Bayesian inference handles ongoing data streams through a fundamental recursive principle:

Today's Posterior is Tomorrow's Prior.

When you receive evidence D_1, you compute the posterior P(H | D_1). When the next piece of evidence D_2 arrives, P(H | D_1) becomes your new prior baseline:

[ Prior P(H) ] ──( Observe D_1 )──► [ Intermediate P(H | D_1) ] ──( Observe D_2 )──► [ Final P(H | D_1 ∩ D_2) ]

Order Independence: Because fraction arithmetic is associative and commutative, updating sequentially step-by-step yields the exact same mathematical result as combining all data into one giant event and updating all at once!

2. The Odds Formulation: Evidence as a Multiplier

While probabilities range between 0 and 1, real-world decision makers often express uncertainty as odds:

Prior Odds = P(H_1) / P(H_0)

When new data D arrives, the Likelihood Ratio (called the Bayes Factor) acts as a direct physical multiplier:

Bayes Factor(D) = P(D | H_1) / P(D | H_0)

This yields the celebrated Odds Form of Bayes' Theorem:

Posterior Odds = Bayes Factor × Prior Odds
  • Bayes Factor > 1: The data pushes belief toward hypothesis H_1.
  • Bayes Factor < 1: The data pushes belief toward hypothesis H_0.
  • Bayes Factor = 1: The data is completely uninformative (noise).

3. The Base-Rate Fallacy (Rare Condition Screening)

One of the most vital insights Bayesian inference provides to basic education is explaining why highly accurate tests produce mostly false alarms for rare conditions.

The Scenario:

A rare defect affects only 1 in 100 manufactured components (P(Defect) = 0.01, so Prior Odds are 1 : 99).
A quality scanner is built with impressive laboratory accuracy:

  • True Positive Rate: P(Alarm | Defect) = 0.90 (Detects 90% of actual defects)
  • False Alarm Rate: P(Alarm | Good) = 0.05 (False alarm on only 5% of good chips)

The Bayes Factor for an Alarm:

Bayes Factor = 0.90 / 0.05 = 18.0×   (Strong evidence for a defect!)

Updating After Alarm 1:

Posterior Odds = 18 × (1 / 99) = 18 / 99 = 2 / 11
Posterior Probability = 2 / (2 + 11) = 2 / 13 ≈ 15.4%

The Surprise: Even after failing a 90%-accurate test, the component is still 84.6% likely to be GOOD! Why? Because the overwhelming majority of the population (99%) is good, so a 5% false alarm rate on that massive majority produces far more false positives than the true positives from the tiny 1% pool.

Updating After Alarm 2 (Second Independent Test Fails):

We use our previous posterior odds (2 : 11) as our new prior:

New Posterior Odds = 18 × (2 / 11) = 36 / 11
New Posterior Probability = 36 / (36 + 11) = 36 / 47 ≈ 76.6%

Now, with two independent confirmations, confidence in the defect jumps past 76%.

4. Continuous Parameter Learning: The Beta-Binomial Transect

What if our hypothesis space is not just two discrete options, but a continuous parameter θ ∈ [0, 1] (such as the unknown bias of a coin)?

On our hyperfinite transect ℝ_ω, continuous parameter learning is straightforward:

  • We start with a uniform prior weight across all N = ω points.
  • Observing a Head multiplies the weight at point θ by θ.
  • Observing a Tail multiplies the weight at point θ by (1 - θ).

After observing a heads and b tails, the weight profile on the transect is proportional to the Beta distribution:

P(θ | a, b) ∝ θ^a · (1 - θ)^b

As sample size grows, the probability mass on the transect automatically concentrates into a narrow, sharp peak centered precisely at the empirical frequency θ = a / (a + b).
