Lecture 1: How Science Learns from Clues
Explanations, Observations & The 3-Stage Bayesian Filter

1. Math Deduction vs. Scientific Discovery

In pure mathematics, when you prove a theorem like 2 + 2 = 4, that truth is locked in forever. No new discovery in physics or chemistry will ever un-prove it. This is called monotonic reasoning: truth only ever accumulates.

But the natural sciences do not work by pure deduction. Scientists cannot peek behind the curtain of nature to see absolute truth. Instead, science is fundamentally non-monotonic:

  • You might see 10,000 white swans and feel almost certain that all swans are white.
  • Then, a single black swan swims by, and your belief in that theory immediately collapses!

In the real world, rational thinkers must be able to change their minds when new evidence appears. Bayesian inference is the mathematical tool that tells us exactly how much our beliefs should change.

2. The Primary Ground: Sample Space (Ω) as the Arena of Reality

To make scientific thinking crystal clear, all of science is anchored on a single fundamental stage: the Sample Space / State Space (Ω).

The Core Question of Inference:
"Given that we witnessed points p ∈ Ω, which candidate distribution h ∈ ℋ gave those points the highest likelihood?"

3. Slicing the Probability Line: The 3-Stage Bayesian Filter

Imagine all 100% of our belief laid out along a unit line from 0 to 1 on our hyperfinite transect. Bayesian updating is a visual 3-stage filter:

Stage 1: The Prior Slices Stage 2: Likelihood Slicing Stage 3: Normalization
Where We Start:
Our competing explanations share the unit line according to our initial belief:
P(H_1) + P(H_2) = 1.000
 Testing Compatibility:
When data D is seen, each slice is shaved down according to its predictive power P(D | H):
Surviving = P(D | H) × P(H)
 Rescaling to 100%:
The surviving mass has shrunk. We divide each survivor by the total remaining width to restore a full 100% belief:
Posterior = Surviving / Total

4. A Concrete Example: The Mars Rover Optical Sensor

An autonomous rover is navigating the surface of Mars in dim twilight. It needs to decide whether a dark shape in its path is an obstacle or just a flat shadow:

  • H_clear (Flat ground / safe): Prior belief P(H_clear) = 70% = 0.70
  • H_rock (Dangerous boulder): Prior belief P(H_rock) = 30% = 0.30

The rover fires a laser pulse. The sensor returns a bright reflection: D = "Flash". We know the sensor's physical specs (Likelihoods):

  • If a rock is really there, it reflects the pulse 90% of the time: P(Flash | H_rock) = 0.90
  • If the ground is flat, dusty glare creates a false flash 15% of the time: P(Flash | H_clear) = 0.15

The Math in 3 Simple Steps:

  1. Calculate the Surviving Slices:
    • Rock survivor: 0.90 × 0.30 = 0.270
    • Clear survivor: 0.15 × 0.70 = 0.105

  2. Find the Total Surviving Width P(Flash):
    • Total = 0.270 + 0.105 = 0.375   (37.5% total chance of seeing a flash)

  3. Normalize Back to 100%:
    • Updated belief in Rock: 0.270 / 0.375 = 72.0%
    • Updated belief in Clear: 0.105 / 0.375 = 28.0%
The Takeaway: Before the flash, the rover thought the rock was unlikely (30%). After observing the clue, its belief jumped to 72%. The explanation that best predicted the clue expanded at the expense of the other.

5. Why This Works on the Hyperfinite Transect

Notice something remarkable about this process: we never divided by zero, and we never had to do calculus integrals.

Because our number line ℝ_ω has a discrete infinitesimal grid step dx = 1/ω = ε > 0:

  • Every possible outcome carries an actual, positive sliver of probability: P(x_k) > 0.
  • Only strictly impossible events (E = ∅) have probability zero.
  • Bayesian inference is always pure, exact arithmetic on the line.
