Lecture 2: The 4-Successor Grid & Probability Amplitudes
From Real Numbers on a Line to Complex Arrows on ℂ_ω

1. Why 1D Numbers Are Not Enough

In classical probability (the 2-successor tree {-, +}), every possibility is assigned a positive real number p ≥ 0 on the 1-dimensional transect ℝ_ω. Probabilities simply add up: if there are two mutually exclusive ways for an event to happen, the total probability is:

P_total = P_1 + P_2 (Classical Addition)

Yet at the atomic scale, light and electrons behave like waves. Waves have peaks and troughs, meaning two waves can collide and cancel each other out completely (destructive interference)!

If two positive probabilities always add together, how can two physical pathways cancel to zero? Answer: Nature does not keep track of probabilities directly; nature keeps track of 2-dimensional amplitude arrows on our complex grid ℂ_ω.

2. The 4-Successor Quad-Tree & The Complex Grid (ℂ_ω)

To allow numbers to rotate and cancel, we upgrade from the 2-successor tree to the 4-successor quad-tree:

Quad-Tree Basis = { +1, -1, +i, -i } (The 4 Compass Directions / Clock Angles)

Every node on this 2D grid is a complex number (an arrow):

z = x + i y = r · e^{i θ}
  • r = |z| = √(x² + y²) is the magnitude / length of the arrow.
  • θ is the phase angle (clock direction) of the wave.

3. The Born Rule: From Complex Arrows to Real Probabilities

In 1926, physicist Max Born discovered the foundational rule connecting quantum complex amplitudes to observable probabilities:

The Born Rule:
The probability P of observing a state with amplitude z = x + iy is the squared length of the amplitude arrow:
P = |z|² = x² + y² = z · z*

Because squared lengths are always real and non-negative (|z|² ≥ 0), this guarantees that every calculated probability is a valid positive number!

4. Wave Interference: Why Waves Can Cancel

In quantum mechanics, when multiple pathways lead to the same outcome, amplitudes add as 2D vectors first, and only then do we square the result:

z_total = z_1 + z_2 ⇒ P_total = |z_1 + z_2|²

Case A: Destructive Interference (Cancellation)

Suppose Path 1 has amplitude z_1 = +0.5 (pointing East) and Path 2 has amplitude z_2 = -0.5 (pointing West, out of phase by 180°):

z_total = (+0.5) + (-0.5) = 0 ⇒ P_total = |0|² = 0% Probability! (Darkness / Zero Detections)

Case B: Constructive Interference (Reinforcement)

Suppose both paths are in phase: z_1 = +0.5 and z_2 = +0.5:

z_total = (+0.5) + (+0.5) = 1.0 ⇒ P_total = |1.0|² = 100% Probability! (Full Signal)

5. The Quantum State Vector

On our hyperfinite complex grid ℂ_ω, a complete quantum state is simply a unit vector of amplitude arrows:

|ψ⟩ = ( z_0, z_1, z_2, ..., z_{ω-1} )^T

The sum of all squared lengths equals 1.000 (total probability is 100%):

∑_{k=0}^{ω-1} |z_k|² = 1.000

In Lecture 3, we see how measuring a quantum state vector is simply projecting an arrow onto a detector axis—the quantum upgrade of the Bayesian filter!
