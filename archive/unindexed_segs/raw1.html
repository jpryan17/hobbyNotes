Formal Statements: The Lecture

"Well hello, all. Look who’s back—it’s Jack."
A collective grunt echoes through the room.
"Hey, it’s okay. Your secret is safe with me if you haven’t been able to stop thinking about propositions since we last hung out in fourth grade.
Look at you all—you're older, wiser, and you've survived a couple of years of standard math since then. But I admit it: those standalone propositions we learned back then are kind of boring. Don’t worry. I am here today to vanquish them to a small, supporting role on a much larger stage. But first..." Jack clears his throat dramatically "...I must set the stage."
Cue eye-rolling and a few audible groans.
"We all know what a collection of objects is, right? And many of you have probably heard the term 'set theory.' But did you know that not all collections are sets? Some collections are actually too big to be sets.
Now, mathematical sets can be really, really big, so this size limit isn't going to cause any problems for us. For our purposes, we can safely think of a set as a collection."
"But how can a collection be too big to be a set?" Jill asks, her face a picture of pure inquisitiveness.
"Hello, Jill, nice to see you again. Well, 'set' is a precise mathematical term—a very important one. A set doesn't just have a definition; it has a whole branch of mathematics devoted to its rules. As it turns out, if you try to group absolutely everything together into a single set, the math breaks. For example, the collection of all possible sets cannot be a set itself. If you try to treat it like one, you run face-first into paradoxes where you can prove contradicting conclusions. It’s all very confusing.
And I’m no expert, Jill. Sometimes, at least until we have built the machinery to see it for ourselves, we just have to trust established mathematical fact as fact."
"Okay, so you just warned us about a massive mathematical trap that we don't even have to worry about?" Jill asks, triggering a wave of amused agreement around the room.
"Ha! I guess I did, Jill. But it highlights a deeper point: a set is more than just a random pile of things. It has strict structural rules, even if those limits are far, far away from what we are doing today.
In the jargon of logicians, a set is a primitive notion. That means we aren't going to waste time trying to formally define it with other words; we will just rely on our natural intuition of what a collection is. What we will formally describe are two set constructors—named the product and the directed-pair—which allow us to build new sets out of existing ones.
To use these constructors, we need some base sets to start with. A great example of a base set is the set of natural numbers, which we denote as $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. Another base set is our binary friend from yesterday: the set of Boolean truth values, $\mathbb{B} = \{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$."

1. The Product Constructor (×)

"Our first constructor is the product. A product is a set whose members are tuples. Think of a tuple as a sequence of slots. Aside from keeping track of which slot is which, the physical order of the slots doesn't change what the tuple is.
Each slot is permanently bound to a specific set, and the value inside that slot must be a member of that set. You can bind multiple slots to the same set, or to completely different sets. The product constructor simply builds the set of all possible combinations of those bound slots.
We denote a product by writing the sets side-by-side, separated by a multiplication symbol. For example, if we want to build a set of tuples with two slots, where both slots are bound to the natural numbers, we write the product as $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$."
"Okay, but what does any of this have to do with a product? Like... multiplication in arithmetic?" Jill interrupts.
"Not much, Jill. To make things worse, mathematicians often refer to the sets inside a constructor as 'product factors.' I guess we just run out of words sometimes and reuse old ones. It’s confusing, but at least now you know the secret code, right?"
A few murmured denials drift back from the back rows.
"Don't worry, the product constructor is eventually used to help describe arithmetic. But first, we need to look at our second constructor."

2. The Directed-Pair Constructor (→)

"A directed-pair is constructed from two sets: the first is called the domain, and the second is called the codomain. We use a directed-pair when we want to formally define a function.
A function is simply a directed-pair combined with a specific rule that assigns exactly one member of the codomain to any member of the domain. By using an arrow to separate the domain and codomain, we can describe standard addition or multiplication of natural numbers as a directed-pair:
$$\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$$
accompanied by a rule for adding or multiplying those tuple values together. Any questions?"
"Can a directed-pair be a domain or a codomain itself?" Jill asks.
"Ah, phenomenal question. Yes, it absolutely can. That is exactly how you start describing a function that processes other functions. But to keep things simple at our level, we are going to enforce a house rule: we won't use directed-pairs as domains or codomains. It keeps our scaffolding nice and flat."
A mostly puzzled but accepting nod goes around the room.

The Convention

"Let’s establish a quick shorthand convention for writing a function. We write the name of the function, a colon, and its directed pair. Then, treating a single base set as a tuple with just one slot, we provide an input tuple labeled with variables, the mapping symbol $\mapsto$, and a rule showing how those variables determine the output.
For addition, it looks like this:
$$\text{add} : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{N}$$
$$(x_1, x_2) \mapsto x_1 + x_2$$
Now, we are in the perfect position to define the core component of all formal science. Are you ready? Ta-da: The Predicate.
A predicate is simply a function whose codomain is locked to the set of Boolean truth values, $\mathbb{B}$. If you feel a bit let down after all that buildup, good! A predicate is beautiful because it is that simple.
A classic example is the 'greater than' predicate, which compares two natural numbers:
$$\text{PG} : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{B}$$
$$(x_1, x_2) \mapsto \begin{cases} \text{true} & \text{if } x_1 > x_2 \\ \text{false} & \text{if } \neg(x_1 > x_2) \end{cases}$$
We can look at an even simpler predicate with only a single slot: checking whether a natural number is less than 10. Treating our domain as a single-slot tuple, its definition looks like this:
$$\text{PL10} : \mathbb{N} \rightarrow \mathbb{B}$$
$$(x) \mapsto \begin{cases} \text{true} & \text{if } x < 10 \\ \text{false} & \text{if } \neg(x < 10) \end{cases}$$
Crucially, a standalone predicate is neither true nor false. It’s just an empty template waiting for inputs. However, we can ask questions about it: 'Is there some natural number less than 10?' or 'Are all natural numbers less than 10?'
The answer to the first question is a definitive yes (true), and the second is a definitive no (false). By asking these questions, we have turned a flexible predicate back into a rigid proposition—a statement that is forced to be true or false."

The Content-Free Guide to Quantifiers

"Well, we have to face them sooner or later; may as well make it sooner, right?"
The class reacts with shifting weight and curious glances.
"We shall face the dreaded backward E and the upside-down A—those symbols for the highbrow phrases 'there exists' and 'for all.' But, tradition and all, we need to get used to them. So let’s decode them and see exactly what’s being said before we connect them to any real-world content:
  • The ∃ Symbol: Means 'there exists,' which means that there is some member of the predicate domain that evaluates to, or assigns, the value true.
  • The ∀ Symbol: Means 'for all,' which means that every single member of the domain evaluates to true.
Logicians seem to be a rather stilted bunch; their language seems a bit stilted, but they mean well, so we live with it, right guys?"

The Table of Strength

"Before we even hook these symbols up to real math rules like 'greater than' or 'less than', the mere sequence of these characters tells us exactly how 'heavy' a statement is. Here is our master cheat sheet for two variables, listed from the absolute strongest claim to the weakest:
Strength Tier Quantifier Order What it actually means Operational Reality
Tier 1 (Strongest) ∀ x ∀ y Everyone and everything: True for absolutely every possible pair. Unyielding
Tier 2 (Strong) ∃ x ∀ y The Master Key: One single, fixed choice works for every combination. Rigid
Tier 3 (Weak) ∀ y ∃ x Custom Fit: Everyone gets a match, but the choice shifts depending on the situation. Flexible
Tier 4 (Weakest) ∃ x ∃ y At least once: Minimum threshold. A single working pair exists somewhere. Permissive
Because the Master Key requires one fixed choice upfront, it automatically forces the Custom Fit scenario to be true. The logical implication goes strictly down the ladder—Tier 2 completely guarantees Tier 3 ($\exists x \forall y \implies \forall y \exists x$)—but it never, ever goes backward.
Now, here is the catch: because this chart tries to make rules about all possible rules without caring about what's inside the brackets, logicians call it Second-Order Logic. It’s the bird's-eye view of the math universe.
We aren't doing that here. We are taking Step 1.
Our curriculum uses First-Order Logic. In our sandbox, we don't make abstract statements about all possible rules. Instead, we keep our feet on the ground, pick a single, concrete relationship—like x > y—and watch how our variables move around inside a real, physical set of numbers.
But the cool thing is that this second-order chart remains our guide. By simply looking at the order of the ∀ and ∃ signs at the very front of your text box, you'll already know exactly how heavy your statement is before the math engine even turns on."

The Visual Matrix Warmup

"Now, before we turn on the simulator and start blindly typing in rules, we need a simple way to visualize what is actually happening under the hood when these quantifiers shift strength.
Imagine we take a two-variable predicate and map it onto a Boolean Matrix—which is just a fancy name for a grid.
  • The rows represent our choices for x.
  • The columns represent our choices for y.
  • Inside the grid cells, we drop a 1 if the combination is True, and a 0 if it is False.
Let's look at how the geometry of this grid visually proves the difference between our two middle strength tiers.

Grid A: Testing the 'Master Key' (∃ x ∀ y)

The statement ∃ x ∀ y claims: 'There is a single master row x that is True for absolutely every column y.'
Visually, this means you are looking for a solid, unbroken horizontal row of 1s stretching completely across the grid:
$$\begin{array}{cc} & \begin{array}{cccc} y_1 & y_2 & y_3 & y_4 \end{array} \\ \begin{array}{c} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4 \end{array} & \left[ \begin{array}{cccc} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array} \right] \end{array}$$
Look at row x₂. Because that master row cuts completely across, it forces every single column it crosses to intersect a 1. This is why the implication flows down the ladder: a solid horizontal row forces every column to be satisfied.

Grid B: Testing the 'Custom Fit' (∀ y ∃ x)

The statement ∀ y ∃ x claims: 'Every single column y has at least one row x where the answer is True.'
Visually, this means every vertical column must have a light turned on, but the 1s do not need to line up. They can be scattered anywhere:
$$\begin{array}{cc} & \begin{array}{cccc} y_1 & y_2 & y_3 & y_4 \end{array} \\ \begin{array}{c} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4 \end{array} & \left[ \begin{array}{cccc} \mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 & \mathbf{1} \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \\ \mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{array} \right] \end{array}$$
Scan this second grid vertically, column by column. Does every column have a 1? Yes, so the statement is True. But look horizontally: is there a single, solid row of 1s? Not even close. They are scattered diagonally.
This is the geometric proof of why the implication can never go backward. You can easily satisfy every vertical column individually without ever creating a single, master horizontal row. Shifting the quantifier order shifts the geometry of truth."

Variable Binding and Scope

"In the simplest case of our single-slot predicate like the less than 10 predicate formalized above, this universal guide makes quantification pretty straightforward to interpret:
  • $\exists x \, [\text{PL10}(x)]$ is a true proposition.
  • $\forall x \, [\text{PL10}(x)]$ is a false proposition.
Everyone tracking so far?"
"What's that x?" Jill asks.
"That is a great question. You see, you don't quantify a predicate directly; you don't even quantify a tuple slot. Rather, you quantify a variable bound to the slot. This variable may be any member of the set to which the slot is bound.
In fact, what we really want to quantify is a predicate expression. If we quantify all the predicates in a predicate expression, we would be dealing with a propositional expression—the same propositional expressions we talked about yesterday, but you might remember some things about them.
But considering a predicate expression complicates things a bit. For example, let $P_1 \equiv \text{greater than 10}$ and $P_2 \equiv \text{less than 20}$, and consider the quantified predicate expressions:
  1. $\exists x, y \, [P_1(x) \wedge P_2(y)]$
  2. $\exists x \, [P_1(x) \wedge P_2(x)]$
Can you see that the first one will always be true, while the second requires a bit more thought because of the way we bind variables to slots? In the first expression, each predicate has its own independent variable. It is easy to find two numbers that will satisfy the expression. On the other hand, how is one number going to satisfy both predicates?
Do you remember what a tautology is? This may help. Let $P \equiv \text{greater than 10}$. Then $\forall x \, [P(x) \vee \neg P(x)]$ is an absolute tautology.
When a predicate, such as the 'greater than' predicate, has more than one slot, things become a bit more complicated, because we might now have to consider the order of mixed quantifiers. For example, let $P \equiv \text{greater than}$, and consider the quantified predicate expressions:
  1. $\forall x \exists y \, [P(x, y)]$
  2. $\exists y \forall x \, [P(x, y)]$
These are completely different propositions. The first is true. The second is false. Some people see this easily. Some don't. I don't; I have to think about it, and eventually I can say, 'oh sure,' like it was always easy to see.
It would be nice to have something like the truth table demo that we used to visualize propositional expressions that we could use to help visualize quantified predicate expressions, wouldn't it? We can't use a truth table because there are way too many possible combinations to enumerate. In addition, the visualization tool needs to understand the predicate rule in order to evaluate all possible predicate values. So we have to keep things simple. The visualization tool is called the formal statement demo. It's pretty simple, but sometimes simple helps."

The Formal Statement Demo

"There are only four built-in predicates:
  • PG5 $\equiv$ greater than 5
  • PL10 $\equiv$ less than 10
  • PG $\equiv$ greater than
  • PL $\equiv$ less than
where the first two predicates are single-slotted, and the last two predicates listed have two slots. Formally, we have four predicates that the user can choose from to make a valid predicate expression.
At this point, there is no difference between a valid predicate expression and a valid propositional expression, and the predicate names can be used as if they label propositions. You are able to construct predicate expressions in the same way you constructed propositional expressions in the truth table demo.
But after you've constructed a valid predicate expression, you must bind each slot in each predicate to a variable. In extreme cases, you may assign the same variable to every slot, or a different variable to each slot, or any combination you choose. Then, you choose a sequence of quantifiers that bind all the variables.
The demo should now be able to determine the truth value of the quantified predicate expression, and provide visual aids to explain why it has this value. This should help those like me who need some time and examples before we can understand a quantified predicate. It's not always obvious."
